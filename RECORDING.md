# Recording the voiceover

Recorded clips for all 18 slides are in `audio/` (read by Nathan, October 2026). The kiosk deck plays a recorded clip for each slide if one is in the `audio/` folder. If a clip is missing, the browser reads that slide's line instead, so you can add recordings one at a time.

## How to record

- Use a phone's voice recorder in a quiet room. Hold it about a hand-span from your mouth.
- Read slowly and with a smile. Leave a second of quiet at the start and end.
- One clip per slide. Each clip can be a different Scout or leader.
- Save each clip with the exact file name below. `.mp3`, `.m4a` (iPhone and most Android voice memos) and `.wav` all work.
- Put the clips in the `audio/` folder and push, or send them to Nathan to add.
- Slide 15 currently says "our callsign". Once the special event callsign is issued, re-record just that clip with the callsign in the radio alphabet, and update line 15 in `NARR`.
- The captions on screen use these words, so stick to the script. If you change a line, update the matching line in `NARR` in `deck.html` too.

**Consent:** this site is public. Only record young people with their parent or carer's permission, and use no full names in the clips.

## Script

| # | File name | Slide | Line to read |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | `01-title.mp3` | Title | Welcome to Jamboree on the Air! This weekend, Scouts all over the world are talking to each other by radio. No phones, no Wi-Fi and no internet. Just radio waves flying through the air. |
| 2 | `02-big-idea.mp3` | The big idea | When you make a phone call, your voice travels through masts, cables and the internet. Ham radio is different. Your voice goes straight from our antenna to theirs, through the air. It even works when the phone network is down. |
| 3 | `03-broadcast-vs-ham.mp3` | Broadcast vs ham radio | There are two kinds of radio. With broadcast radio, like the music stations in the car, one station talks and everyone else just listens. Ham radio is two-way. Both stations can talk and listen, taking turns, just like a walkie-talkie. |
| 4 | `04-press-to-talk.mp3` | Press. Talk. Let go. | This is the magic button. It's called PTT, which means push to talk. First, press and hold the button. Then talk clearly, about a hand-span from the microphone. When you've finished, say over, and let go, so you can hear the reply. |
| 5 | `05-voice-journey.mp3` | The journey of your voice | So what happens when you talk? Your voice makes the air wobble. The microphone turns the wobbles into a tiny electric signal. The radio puts it onto a radio wave, and the antenna sends it flying. Their radio catches it and plays your voice. All at the speed of light! |
| 6 | `06-riding-the-wave.mp3` | Your voice rides a radio wave | Your voice can't travel very far on its own. So the radio puts it on a super-fast radio wave, like a parcel in a delivery van. At the other end, their radio unpacks the parcel, and out comes your voice. |
| 7 | `07-wave-sizes.mp3` | Long waves, short waves | Radio waves come in different sizes. HF waves are really long, from the length of a bus to a football pitch. VHF and UHF waves are much shorter, from as tall as a door to as long as your arm. Longer waves can travel further. |
| 8 | `08-vhf-uhf.mp3` | VHF & UHF | VHF and UHF waves travel in straight lines, like a torch beam. They're great for talking across town. But hills and tall buildings get in the way. A repeater on top of a hill can catch the signal and send it on. |
| 9 | `09-hf.mp3` | HF | HF waves can go right round the world! High above the clouds is a layer called the ionosphere. HF waves bounce off it, back down to the ground, and up again, all the way to Scouts in other countries. The Sun changes the layer, so it's different in the day and at night. |
| 10 | `10-alphabet.mp3` | The radio alphabet | On a crackly radio, letters like B, D and P all sound the same. So radio operators use special words instead. A is Alpha, B is Bravo, C is Charlie. Scout is Sierra, Charlie, Oscar, Uniform, Tango. Can you spell your name? |
| 11 | `11-radio-words.mp3` | Radio shorthand | Radio operators have their own words. CQ means calling anyone. Over means your turn. Roger means I understood. Say again means please repeat. QTH means where I am. And 73 means best wishes, goodbye! |
| 12 | `12-signal-report.mp3` | Signal report | When you talk to another station, tell them how well you can hear them. The first number is how easy they are to understand, from one to five. The second is how strong they are, from one to nine. Five and nine means loud and clear! |
| 13 | `13-j-code.mp3` | The J-Code | Scouts have their own code, called the J-Code, so we can chat even if we don't speak the same language. JWN means my name is. JHO means I am this many years old. Add an X to ask a question. JWNX means what's your name? |
| 14 | `14-j-code-chat.mp3` | A J-Code chat | Here's Sam from England chatting with Paco from Venezuela. They don't speak the same language, but with the J-Code they can swap their names, their ages and even the weather. Happy JOTA-JOTI! |
| 15 | `15-script-1.mp3` | Radio script part 1 | Here's how to make a contact. First, we call: CQ Jamboree, this is our callsign. When someone answers, say hello, give them a signal report and tell them your first name. Remember to spell it out in the radio alphabet! |
| 16 | `16-script-2.mp3` | Radio script part 2 | Now have a chat. Tell them your age, your Scout section and what the weather is like. When you've finished, say thank you, Happy JOTA-JOTI, and 73. Then we write the contact in the logbook. |
| 17 | `17-stay-safe.mp3` | What to say on air | Stay safe on air. It's great to share your first name, your age and what you like doing in Scouts. Keep your surname, address, school and phone number private. A licensed radio operator will be with you the whole time. |
| 18 | `18-your-turn.mp3` | Your turn | Now it's your turn! Ask a leader at the radio table to have a go. While you wait, practise spelling your name in the radio alphabet. Good luck, and 73! |
